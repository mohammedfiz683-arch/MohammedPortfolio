Rani no Hajiro

The Hidden Dimensions of Queen's Tomb

ABOUT THE SITE

Rani no Hajiro is a historic site in the old city of Ahmedabad — the resting place of queens from the Gujarat Sultanate era, built around 1445. The marble tombs carry carvings that blend Hindu, Jain and Islamic design. Some families still live beside the tombs and care for them: a quiet space with a strong connection to the past.

THE MARKET AROUND IT

Right outside the tomb complex is a busy, colourful market known for women's clothing, jewellery and garba outfits during festivals, along with small stalls selling mukhwas. People from across the city come here, especially around Navratri — keeping the heritage alive through everyday activity.

THE RESEARCH QUESTION

"How does the Rani no Hajiro market contribute to the cultural identity of Old Ahmedabad, and how do social and economic factors impact its traditional essence and long-term viability?"

Five Themes

Nostalgia

Vendors and customers value the heritage and the traditional shopping experience.

Uncertainty

Vendors face seasonal fluctuations and competition from online shopping.

Frustration

Infrastructure issues like flooding, congestion and waste management affect daily life.

Determination

Vendors and locals work hard to keep the market alive despite these challenges.

Connection

The market is more than a place to shop — it brings people together.

Insights and Observations

Observation

Vendors were wary that we might be reporters rather than students, and that we could alert authorities — their stalls are technically illegal, and the government has filed complaints against them.

Insight

Distrust of outsiders comes from ongoing eviction threats. Vendors depend on loyal customers, but fear that media or official attention could lead to displacement — visibility in informal economies is both vital and risky.

Observation

Although it's a seasonal market, vendors operate year-round because they depend on devoted, generational customers.

Insight

Generational ownership makes stability matter more than expansion. Their inherited trade is their main livelihood, showing how tradition and necessity shape business decisions.

Observation

The government offers little support in maintaining the heritage; caretaker families carry sole responsibility and haven't pushed back, even after their house was demolished.

Insight

With their home gone and family members unwell, the caretakers have no choice but to stay. Leaving would mean losing the only shelter they have — the tomb they maintain. For them, heritage preservation isn't a job but a means of survival.

OUTCOME: THE ZINE

A zine that translates our research into an accessible visual narrative.

Bringing together personas, spatial mapping and the everyday rhythms of the place into a single tactile object.

Why a Zine?

A zine let us present research in a way that's accessible and engaging, rather than locking it into a formal academic report.
